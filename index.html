How much water, energy and carbon does AI use?

AI runs in data centres. They use electricity, and can use water for cooling and for making that electricity. This page adds up published AI examples at the number of questions you choose, over a year. It does not measure your actual requests.

These are estimates built from published studies, not measurements of your own use. Every number lists where it comes from.

    Why there is no water comparison: the Google example counts on-site cooling water; the older example counts cooling plus power-plant water. A burger's water footprint is thousands of litres in total, and about 94% of that is rain that fell on fields and grass. They are different kinds of water, so setting one beside the other would mislead. Water use also matters locally: a litre in a dry place has a different impact from a litre where water is plentiful.

    Why there is no share or total: in the published examples, the AI figure counts data centres only, the video figure also counts your device and the network, and the beef figure counts a food supply chain. Adding them up or giving percentages would suggest a fair split of one footprint, which these numbers cannot support. The comparison charts in section 2 show each figure on its own, with its boundary.

    Not counted (in the published examples): training the AI model, making the chips, your own device and the network. Google's water figure also leaves out the water used at power plants. These omissions mean this is not a complete life-cycle footprint. The published per-prompt examples are not lower bounds for every chatbot or every request.

    How the Google numbers were made

    The 0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL come from a paper written by Google staff. They are not independently verified: Google's own blog post says the data and claims have not been verified by an independent third party. Google found that the energy per prompt is very uneven, so it reports a median prompt, not an average. It worked out each Gemini model's average energy per prompt, ranked the models, and took the one serving the middle prompt. The paper does not publish an average, so a real mix of prompts could use more or less than 0.24 Wh each. The water is not metered per prompt. Google multiplied the energy for each prompt (leaving out the data centre's own overhead energy) by the previous year's fleet-wide water-use rate for cooling, 1.15 litres per kWh. Where a data centre uses little or no water for cooling, the real figure differs.

    Words used on this page
    Prompt
    One question or request you type to an AI chatbot.
    Wh and kWh (electricity)
    A watt-hour (Wh) is a small amount of electricity: a 10 W LED bulb uses 10 Wh in an hour. 1,000 Wh make a kilowatt-hour (kWh), which is what home electricity bills use.
    Greenhouse gases and CO₂e
    Things we make or do release gases that warm the planet, such as carbon dioxide and methane. "CO₂e" (carbon dioxide equivalent) is one number that turns all of them into the amount of carbon dioxide that would warm the planet the same. It lets different things be compared.
    Blue, green and grey water
    Blue is water taken from rivers and underground. Green is rain that crops and grass soak up. Grey is the water needed to dilute pollution. Most of the water behind beef is green.
    Power-plant water
    Water that evaporates at power stations to make the electricity a data centre uses. It is separate from the water the data centre itself uses for cooling. Google's published figure leaves it out; the older estimate includes it. Your own water number is not assumed to do either.
    Not counted
    Training the AI model and making its chips also use energy and water. The published examples here do not include them, because no published figure that fits was found. Numbers you enter yourself are not assumed to include or exclude them.
    Squashed (logarithmic) scale
    A chart where each equal extra length means "multiplied by the same factor" (for example ten times) instead of "the same amount added". The smallest bar is given a short starting length so it can be seen. It makes small things visible, but bar lengths no longer show true ratios, so read the numbers. A zero has no bar.
    Efficiency gain and growth
    When each use gets cheaper, people may use more of it, so the total can still rise. Economists call this the Jevons paradox.
